Life Insurance After Cancer: What You Need to Know

T
Tanweer Hussain
Protection Expert
3 June 2026 11 min read

Getting life insurance after cancer is possible for many people. Whether it is available, and on what terms, depends on the type and stage of the cancer, how long ago treatment ended, and whether you are in remission. The outcome varies significantly between insurers, which is why working with a whole-of-market broker matters particularly for anyone with a cancer history.

This is not a topic where a comparison website helps very much. Most will decline at the point of the health questions, before a human being has looked at your application. The options that exist for people with a cancer diagnosis or history are found by going further than that.

Can You Get Life Insurance After Cancer?

Yes, in many cases. A cancer history does not automatically mean you cannot get life insurance. What it means is that the application requires more information, and the outcome depends on the specifics of your situation rather than a yes or no rule.

People who have had cancer and are now in remission frequently obtain life insurance, sometimes at standard rates and sometimes at a higher premium to reflect the insurer's assessment of risk. Others are offered cover with a specific exclusion relating to their cancer. Some applications are postponed, with the insurer asking the applicant to reapply after a certain period. And some are declined.

Which of those outcomes applies in your case is not something any guide can tell you in advance. It depends on too many individual factors. What this post can do is explain what those factors are, what to expect from the process, and why the route you take to apply matters.

What Insurers Consider When You Have a Cancer History

When assessing a life insurance application from someone with a cancer history, insurers typically look at:

  • Type of cancer: Some cancers carry a higher recurrence risk than others. Certain early-stage skin cancers, for example, are treated very differently from a secondary cancer with metastasis.
  • Stage and grade at diagnosis: The more advanced the cancer was, the more carefully an insurer will assess the ongoing risk.
  • Treatment received: Surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and ongoing hormone therapy are all relevant. Whether treatment is complete or ongoing is significant.
  • Time since treatment ended: The longer the gap between the end of treatment and the application, the better the position is generally. For some cancers, insurers may look for two to five years clear before offering standard terms.
  • Current status: Whether you are in active treatment, in remission, or have received an all-clear affects what cover is available and at what cost.
  • Other health factors: Age, smoking status, family history, and the sum assured requested are all part of the overall picture.

No two applications are identical. The same cancer type can produce different outcomes depending on the individual combination of these factors.

What the Possible Outcomes Are

When a life insurance application is assessed after a cancer diagnosis, there are typically four possible outcomes:

  • Standard terms: Cover is offered at the same premium as someone without a cancer history. This is more likely with certain low-risk cancers, longer remission periods, or lower-stage diagnoses.
  • Loaded premium: Cover is offered, but at a higher premium to reflect the additional risk the insurer is taking on. The degree of loading varies widely between insurers for the same condition.
  • Exclusion: Cover is offered, but with a clause excluding claims related to cancer. This means the policy pays out for other causes of death but not cancer.
  • Postponement: The insurer asks you to reapply after a certain period, usually once more time has passed since treatment.
  • Decline: No offer of cover is made at this time. This does not mean cover will never be available, but an application to a different insurer with different underwriting criteria may produce a different result.

The same application submitted to different insurers can produce different outcomes. This is one of the clearest reasons why approaching the whole market, rather than one or two providers, is worth doing.

Life Insurance During Active Treatment

If you are currently in active treatment for cancer, obtaining new life insurance is significantly more difficult. Most standard insurers will not offer cover during active treatment, and many will postpone the application until treatment has concluded and a period of remission has passed.

Two things are worth knowing in this situation. First, if you already have life insurance taken out before your diagnosis, that policy remains in force. The insurer cannot cancel or alter it because of a diagnosis made after the policy was issued. Second, if you are aged 50 to 80, guaranteed acceptance over-50 plans are available without medical questions, though these carry lower sum assured limits and waiting periods before a full payout applies in the early months.

Some life insurance policies include a terminal illness benefit, which allows a payout to be made during the policyholder's lifetime when a terminal prognosis is confirmed. If you have an existing policy, it is worth checking whether this applies.

Life Insurance After Cancer in Remission

Remission significantly improves the position for many people seeking life insurance after a cancer diagnosis. The general principle is that the longer the period since the end of treatment, the lower the assessed risk, and the more favourable the terms available are likely to be.

This is not a universal rule. Some cancers have a lower recurrence risk after a relatively short remission period and may be assessed at near-standard rates relatively quickly. Others, particularly those diagnosed at a more advanced stage, may require a longer clear period before an insurer is willing to offer cover on acceptable terms.

Life insurance after breast cancer, for example, is commonly available for people in longer-term remission, though the terms will vary based on the stage and type of breast cancer, the treatment received, and how long remission has lasted. There is no single answer that covers all cases.

If a first application is declined or postponed, it is worth revisiting after further time in remission. An outcome that is not possible today may be available in a year or two.

What Happens to Life Insurance You Already Have

If you have a life insurance policy in place before a cancer diagnosis, the policy continues on its existing terms. The insurer cannot cancel it, increase your premium, or add exclusions because of a condition that developed after the policy was issued.

This is worth knowing because it reinforces the case for arranging life insurance before any health issues arise, while standard terms are available. A policy arranged at 32 when you are healthy remains in force at the same terms regardless of what happens to your health later.

It also means that reviewing cover at the point of a diagnosis is about understanding what you have, not about having it changed or removed.

Disclosure: The One Thing You Cannot Get Wrong

Every life insurance application requires you to answer health questions accurately and fully. "Disclosure" sounds like it belongs in a legal thriller, but it is essentially just this: tell the truth about your health history on the application form.

This matters because life insurance is a contract that relies on the information you provide. If material information was withheld or misrepresented at the point of application, an insurer may be entitled to void the policy and refuse a claim, even if the claim is unrelated to the condition that was not disclosed.

There are two practical implications. First, do not be tempted to omit a cancer history on an application in the hope of obtaining a lower premium. The risk is that a claim is made and the payout does not happen, which is the worst possible outcome. Second, when an insurer asks health questions, answer them thoroughly. If you are uncertain whether something is relevant, include it.

A broker can help navigate health questions on an application and ensure the information is presented clearly and accurately, which can itself reduce the risk of a disclosure-related problem later.

Why a Broker Matters More Here Than Almost Anywhere Else

For most life insurance applications, using a broker and going direct to an insurer produce similar outcomes. The main benefit of a broker is advice and access to the full market.

For people with a cancer history, the case for using a whole-of-market broker is considerably stronger. The reasons are practical:

  • Comparison websites apply automated criteria and typically decline at the health questions stage without any human review of the application.
  • Applying directly to one insurer means being assessed against one set of underwriting criteria. A decline from one insurer is not a decline from the market.
  • Specialist underwriters and panels that are not available directly can be accessed through a broker with the right market connections.
  • Different insurers assess the same condition differently. For a given cancer type and remission period, the terms available can vary significantly across the market.

TopQuote is a whole-of-market life insurance broker. For applications that involve a cancer history, working with an adviser who can approach the whole market and present the application accurately is particularly worthwhile.

What About Other Health Conditions?

The principles that apply to life insurance after cancer apply equally to other serious health conditions. Life insurance after a stroke, life insurance after a heart attack, and life insurance with diabetes are all assessed on similar grounds: the condition itself, when it occurred, severity, current management, and the time that has passed. In each case, a first decline from one insurer does not close the market, and a whole-of-market approach is the most effective route to finding what is available.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you get life insurance after cancer?

Yes, in many cases. Whether cover is available, and on what terms, depends on the type and stage of cancer, when treatment ended, and how long you have been in remission. The outcome varies between insurers, so approaching the whole market rather than a single provider gives the best chance of finding appropriate cover.

How long after cancer treatment can you get life insurance?

There is no single waiting period that applies to all cancers. For some types, insurers may consider applications after two years in remission. For others, a longer clear period may be required. The position improves over time in most cases, and an application that is postponed today may be viable in a year or two.

Can I get life insurance after breast cancer?

Life insurance after breast cancer is available for many people, particularly those with a longer remission period and earlier-stage diagnosis. Terms vary depending on the type of breast cancer, the treatment received, and how long since treatment ended. A whole-of-market broker can outline what is likely to be available for your specific situation.

Will my existing life insurance be affected by a cancer diagnosis?

No. A life insurance policy you have in place before a diagnosis continues on its existing terms. Insurers cannot cancel or alter a policy because of a condition that developed after it was issued. Your existing cover remains in force.

What if my life insurance application is declined because of cancer?

A decline from one insurer does not mean the market is closed. Different insurers assess the same condition differently. A whole-of-market broker can identify which insurers are most likely to consider the application and on what terms. In some cases, reapplying after a further period in remission also improves the position.

Do I have to declare cancer on a life insurance application?

Yes. Life insurance applications require full and accurate disclosure of your health history. Failing to declare a cancer history is considered non-disclosure and can result in a claim being declined even if the claim is unrelated to the cancer. Accuracy at application protects the policy's validity.

Can I get critical illness cover after cancer?

Critical illness cover after a cancer diagnosis is more difficult to obtain than life insurance, and cancer is likely to be excluded from any policy that is offered. Some insurers may decline altogether. That said, a whole-of-market broker can identify what is available and whether any terms are appropriate for your situation.
