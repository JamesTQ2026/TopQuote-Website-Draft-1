Income protection and mental health

Yes, income protection can cover mental health conditions. It does not work from a list of named conditions: it covers most illnesses that leave you unable to work, depending on the type of policy and its definition of incapacity.[1] Critical illness cover generally does not pay out for mental health conditions.

Two things follow from it. The definition of incapacity matters more here than any other setting, and a mental health history disclosed at application is assessed rather than automatically excluded.

This page is general information about how this type of cover works. It is not advice, not medical advice, and not personal to your circumstances. It describes no particular insurer terms and quotes no prices. No product, insurer or service is being promoted here, and nothing on this page is an invitation to buy anything. Numbered references link to the source for each statement, and all sources are listed in the appendix.

Does income protection cover mental health?

Critical illness cover pays a lump sum if the policyholder is diagnosed with a prescribed serious illness or medical condition.[2] Mental health conditions do not generally appear among those prescribed conditions.

The Association of British Insurers also defines a term used in several critical illness definitions, permanent neurological deficit with persisting clinical symptoms, and states that symptoms of psychological or psychiatric origin are not covered by it.[3]

Income protection is built differently. It asks a single question: are you able to do the work the policy defines. A condition that stops you doing it is within scope whatever its nature. On mental health specifically, we look at whether it covers mental health.

Own occupation vs any occupation for mental health claims

Policies use one of three tests for being unable to work: own occupation, suited occupation or any occupation. Own occupation is the most likely to pay out and costs the most. How the three definitions compare.

For a mental health condition this distinction is particularly consequential. A condition can make a specific demanding role impossible while leaving someone capable of other work in principle. Under own occupation cover that is the situation being insured. Under any occupation cover it may not be. The strongest definition is covered in our guide to own occupation cover.

Applying with a mental health history

Applications ask about your health, and that includes mental health. The history is assessed like any other part of a medical record.

Guidance sets out what affects the cost: your age, your occupation, whether you smoke or have smoked, the percentage of income you want to cover, the deferred period, the range of illnesses and injuries covered, and your current health, weight and family medical history.[1]

The realistic outcomes are cover on standard terms, cover at a higher premium, cover with an exclusion, or a decline or postponement. Which applies depends on the condition, how long ago it was diagnosed, how it has been treated, whether it has caused absence from work, and how stable things have been.

Insurers assess the same information differently, so a decision from one is not a decision from the market.

Do antidepressants affect an application?

Applications ask about medication, so it forms part of what is assessed. It does not automatically mean a decline.

What tends to matter is the surrounding picture rather than the prescription on its own: how long you have been taking it, whether the dose has been stable, whether there has been time off work, and whether there is any other history alongside it.

This is a question for the insurer or an adviser dealing with your application, and the answer varies. What it is not is a reason to leave the information out.

Mental health exclusions on income protection

If terms are offered with an exclusion attached, check exactly what it removes.

Because income protection pays out when you cannot work, not on a named diagnosis, an exclusion removes claims arising from a condition whatever form the eventual inability to work takes.[1] An exclusion framed around mental health conditions therefore reaches further than the same words would on a policy built around a condition list.

Given that mental health conditions are among the reasons people are unable to work, an exclusion for them removes a meaningful part of what the policy would otherwise do. Ask the insurer to spell out what would and would not be covered before accepting terms. If you already have a condition, we look at income protection with a pre-existing condition.

Disclosure, which is the difficult part

Be accurate about your medical history. When you claim, the insurer will check it, and if you did not answer truthfully or accurately, or did not disclose something, you might not get a payout.[1]

Under the Consumer Insurance (Disclosure and Representations) Act 2012 there is a duty to take reasonable care not to make a misrepresentation to the insurer before the contract is entered into. Where a misrepresentation was careless, a scale of proportionate remedies applies; where deliberate or reckless, the insurer may avoid the contract and refuse the claim.[4]

Insurers see GP records at claim stage. A history disclosed at application is a history the insurer has already assessed and priced. A history left out is a problem that surfaces at the worst possible moment, for whoever is dealing with the claim.

Disclosing a mental health history can feel exposing.

If you are unwell now

An application made while a condition is active or being investigated is more likely to be postponed than declined outright, and a postponement is a delay rather than a permanent answer.

If you already hold a policy, a condition developing after it started does not change its terms, and it is not pre-existing for that policy.[1] The claim question is whether you meet the definition of being unable to work.

The thing not to do is cancel cover you already hold. Replacing it later means applying again with a longer history.

Benefits and clinical questions

Questions about benefits entitlement, and clinical questions about a diagnosis, are outside what a page about insurance can usefully answer.

Anything about your own health belongs with your GP or the team treating you. Nothing on this page is medical advice.

What to check

  • Which definition of incapacity applies, since that decides whether a claim succeeds.
  • Whether any exclusion has been applied, and precisely what it removes.
  • The deferred period, and what would support you during it.
  • What is excluded generally, since definitions and exclusions vary between insurers.[1]
  • What cover exists through an employer, and how long it would last.[1]
  • That everything asked on the application has been answered fully.

You can read more about what income protection is and income protection vs critical illness.

Things to keep in mind

  • Income protection covers most illnesses that leave you unable to work.[1]
  • The definition of incapacity decides whether a claim succeeds.[1]
  • An exclusion removes claims arising from a condition, which is broader than removing a named diagnosis.
  • It replaces part of your income, not all of it.[1]
  • A claim may be reduced or refused if the information given was not accurate.[4]
  • Cover ends if you stop paying your premiums.
