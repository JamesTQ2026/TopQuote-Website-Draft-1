Decreasing term life insurance explained

Decreasing term life insurance is a policy where the amount you have insured your life for reduces steadily every year to the end of the term, in line with a repayment mortgage, while the premium stays the same.[2]

This page is general information about how a type of life cover works. It is not advice and it is not personal to your circumstances. No product, insurer or service is being promoted here, and nothing on this page is an invitation to buy anything. Numbered references link to the source for each statement, and all sources are listed in the appendix.

It is usually bought alongside a repayment mortgage, often during the mortgage application itself.

What does decreasing term life insurance mean?

Two parts. The term is the fixed length of time you choose at the start, such as five, ten or 25 years.[1] Decreasing means the amount that would be paid out falls each year rather than staying the same.[2]

It is designed to be used with repayment mortgages, where the outstanding loan decreases over time.[1] The idea is that the cover falls roughly as the debt falls.

How does decreasing term life insurance work?

You choose the starting amount of cover and the length of the term, then answer questions about your health, your lifestyle and your family medical history. The insurer sets a premium based on those answers, your age and the cover you asked for.[1]

If you die during the term, a claim can be made and the amount of cover in place at that point is paid. The premium does not fall as the cover falls: it stays the same throughout.

If you are still alive at the end of the term, the policy ends and nothing is paid. Cover also ends if you stop paying the premiums.

A decreasing term life insurance example

Someone takes out cover of £200,000 over 25 years alongside a repayment mortgage. In the early years a claim would pay close to the full £200,000. By year 20 the amount payable is far smaller, because the cover has been reducing each year. In the final year it is smaller again, and at the end of the term it stops entirely.

The premium in that example is the same in year 24 as it was in year one.

Decreasing or level term life insurance?

Level term cover pays a lump sum that stays the same throughout the term.[1] Decreasing cover falls each year. Everything else about the product follows from that.

Because the sum insured on a decreasing policy falls year by year, the total amount of cover it provides across the term is smaller than a level policy starting at the same figure. What that means for the price you are quoted depends on the insurer and on your own circumstances, so the only reliable comparison is a quote for both on the same starting sum and the same term.

What is the difference between level term and decreasing life insurance?

The payout, and nothing else. Both run for a fixed term. Both pay nothing if you outlive it. Both stop if the premiums stop. Level pays the same amount whenever a claim happens; decreasing pays less as the years pass.

Should I get level term or decreasing life insurance?

It depends what the money is for.

Decreasing cover is built around a debt that reduces, which in practice means a repayment mortgage.[1] Level cover suits a need that does not reduce: an interest only mortgage, money for children while they are young, ongoing household bills, or a set sum you want to leave behind.

A point worth checking in your own policy documents: a decreasing policy reduces on a schedule set by the insurer, not by tracking the balance on your mortgage account. How that schedule is calculated, and how closely it is likely to follow your actual debt, is set out in the documents the insurer gives you.

How much does decreasing term life insurance cost?

The premium depends on your age, your health, your lifestyle, whether you smoke, your family medical history, the length of the policy and your occupation, since a higher risk job might push premiums up. The amount of cover you buy also affects the price.[1]

Prices vary between insurers, so it is worth comparing. Any price shown before the medical questions are answered is an estimate, and the insurer sets the final terms once it has assessed the application.

How much cover do you need?

The amount will depend on any debts, your mortgage or rent, the number of dependants you have, and your take home pay or income from other sources.[1]

Check what you already have before deciding. Employee packages often include death in service benefit linked to salary, though that cover ends if you leave the employer.[1]

Decreasing term life insurance: pros and cons

  • It is shaped around a reducing debt, so the cover falls as a repayment mortgage falls.
  • The premium stays the same for the whole term even though the cover reduces.
  • The amount payable in the later years is much smaller than at the start, so it is a poor fit for a need that does not shrink.
  • It reduces on the insurer schedule rather than tracking your actual mortgage balance, so a claim may not match the debt exactly.
  • It pays nothing if you are alive at the end of the term, and there is no refund of premiums.
  • Cover ends if the premiums stop being paid.[1]

Decreasing term life insurance and your mortgage

It is not a legal requirement to have life insurance when you get a mortgage. You might choose it if you want your dependants to have enough money to keep the home when you die.[1]

The fit depends on the mortgage type. On a repayment mortgage the balance reduces over time, which is what this cover is designed around. On an interest only mortgage the capital does not reduce, so cover that falls each year leaves a growing gap against a debt that stays the same.

The payout goes to the people entitled to it, not directly to the lender, so it is for them to decide what to do with the money unless a separate arrangement is in place.

Does decreasing term life insurance have a cash value?

Cash in value means the amount you get if you cash in an investment.[2] Term protection policies of this kind are designed to pay out on a valid claim rather than to build up a fund, and are normally described in the policy documents as having no cash in value at any time. Check yours.

If you cancel, you will not get a refund of premiums already paid, and you cannot reinstate the policy once it is cancelled.[1]

Can I cancel decreasing term life insurance?

Yes. A consumer has a right to cancel a pure protection contract without penalty and without giving any reason within 30 days.[4]

After that you can still ask the insurer to cancel at any time, but there are usually no cancellation fees and no refund of what you have already paid, and you will not be able to reinstate the policy once it has gone.[1]

Two things worth knowing before you cancel anything. Replacement cover might be more expensive, because prices generally increase with age, and pre-existing medical conditions might not be covered by a new policy. If you do switch, do not cancel the existing policy until the replacement is fully set up and the first payment has been made.[1]

If the reason for cancelling is that you cannot afford it, contact the insurer instead. Insurers must support customers in financial difficulty, and they may be able to adjust the cover or the payments rather than lose it altogether.[1]

What to do if you think a policy was mis-sold

Complain to the business that sold it to you first. It should have the chance to put things right, and for most complaints it has up to eight weeks to respond.[5]

If you are unhappy with the response, or the time limit passes without one, you can ask the Financial Ombudsman Service to investigate. There is no charge for using the service, and you do not need to pay anyone to represent you, such as a lawyer or a claims management company.[5]

Time limits apply. You usually have six months from the firm final response to go to the Ombudsman, and it needs to be within six years of being sold the product, or three years from when you noticed something was wrong.[6]

Claims management companies will usually take a large share of any compensation, up to 30 per cent, for following a process you can carry out yourself at no cost. If the firm responsible has gone out of business, the Financial Services Compensation Scheme may be able to help instead.[6]

Joint decreasing term life insurance

Decreasing cover can be written as a joint policy covering two people. Joint cover is usually more affordable than two separate single policies, but it only pays out on the first death, whereas two single policies would pay out on each death.[1]

On a joint decreasing policy that matters twice over: after a claim the policy ends, and the survivor is left without cover at an older age.

Adding critical illness cover

Critical illness cover can be bought alongside life cover for an additional cost.[7] On a decreasing policy the critical illness element normally reduces in the same way as the life cover, so check the policy documents for how it is calculated.

It pays out only on the conditions listed in the policy, where the diagnosis meets the definition written into it, so many illnesses are not included, and definitions and exclusions vary between insurers.[1]

Why might a claim be refused?

Under the Consumer Insurance (Disclosure and Representations) Act 2012 you have a duty to take reasonable care not to make a misrepresentation to the insurer before the contract is entered into.[3]

If that duty is broken and the insurer shows it would not have offered the contract at all, or would have offered it only on different terms, it has a remedy. Where the misrepresentation was careless a scale of proportionate remedies applies; where it was deliberate or reckless the insurer may avoid the contract and refuse the claim.[3] Insurers also check medical history at claim stage.[1]

Things to keep in mind

  • The amount payable falls every year while the premium stays the same.
  • The reduction follows the insurer schedule, not your actual mortgage balance.
  • Cover ends if you stop paying your premiums.
  • Term protection policies are normally described as having no cash in value at any time.
  • A claim may be refused if the information given was not accurate.[3]
  • If you are alive at the end of the term, the policy ends and nothing is paid.
