How long does a life insurance claim take to pay out?

Usually weeks rather than days, and the biggest factor is a decision made when the policy was set up.

Most life insurance claims take weeks rather than days. The Financial Conduct Authority reviewed how 15 life insurers handle bereavement claims and found that the majority of firms averaged between 53 and 122 days on a term insurance claim, measured from a claimant first making contact to the money being paid.[1]

What moves that number most is not the insurer. It is whether the policy was written in trust, and whether the paperwork is easy to find.

This page is general information about how life insurance claims are paid. It is not advice and it is not personal to your circumstances. No product, insurer or service is being promoted here, and nothing on this page is an invitation to buy anything. Numbered references link to the source for each statement, and all sources are listed at the foot of the page.

Average life insurance claim times

The review covered 15 firms representing over 75 per cent of the life protection market, and measured the whole journey rather than just the part after the forms arrive.[1]

  • Term insurance: most firms averaged between 53 and 122 days. Claim values tend to be higher and the checks more thorough, often including requests for further medical evidence.[1]
  • Whole of life: about half of firms settled within 53 days on average.[1]
  • Over 50s plans: about half settled within 20 days on average. Sums are usually smaller and many firms accept these claims without further assessment.[1]
  • Group cover through an employer: the average time to process a claim once received was within 36 days.[1]

Two things to hold alongside those figures. They are averages across firms, not commitments by any insurer. And the same review found most firms were unable to meet some of their own service standards during the period examined, so claimants are likely to be experiencing delays.[1] If you have read somewhere that claims are paid in three to five working days, that is the time some insurers take to release money once everything is in place. It is not how long the process takes from the beginning.

Writing the policy in trust and claim times

Whether the policy was written in trust.

If it was, the money sits outside the estate and can be paid to the people named without waiting for probate.[3] If it was not, the insurer pays into the estate, and the estate has to be dealt with before anyone can receive it.[6]

That is a decision made when the policy is arranged, often years before anyone thinks about claiming. It is also the single most useful thing to check on a policy you already hold.

How to find out which you have

  • Look for a trust deed or a nomination form alongside the policy documents.
  • Check whether anyone is named as a trustee.
  • If you cannot tell, the insurer can confirm it.

A trust is a legal arrangement rather than an insurance one, and it is difficult to undo once set up, so it is worth understanding properly rather than assuming it is always the right answer.

What actually slows a claim down

The regulator identified the common causes, and most of them are practical rather than suspicious.

  • Waiting for documents. Firms reported delays in returning death certificates, grants of probate and claim forms as a frequent cause of longer journey times.[1]
  • Further medical evidence. More likely on higher value claims, and it means waiting on a third party such as a GP practice.[1]
  • Probate. Where the money goes into the estate, the estate process has to run its course first.
  • No will. That makes the estate process longer before anyone has authority to act.
  • The policy not being found. Nobody can claim on a policy they do not know exists.

None of those is the insurer being difficult. Most of them are fixable in advance, which is the point of the section further down.

What happens if the insurer investigates

An insurer can look again at what was disclosed when the policy was taken out. Under the Consumer Insurance (Disclosure and Representations) Act 2012 there is a duty to take reasonable care not to make a misrepresentation to the insurer before the contract is entered into.[4]

If that duty was broken and the insurer shows it would not have offered the policy at all, or only on different terms, it has a remedy. Where the misrepresentation was careless, a scale of proportionate remedies applies, which can mean a reduced payment rather than none. Where it was deliberate or reckless, the insurer may avoid the contract and refuse the claim.[4]

Claims are not refused because someone forgot to post a form. They are affected by what was said on the application, years earlier, by someone who is no longer around to explain it.

Your legal right to timely payment

This is a legal right, not a customer service promise.

Section 13A of the Insurance Act 2015, inserted by the Enterprise Act 2016, makes it an implied term of every insurance contract that if you make a claim, the insurer must pay any sums due within a reasonable time.[2]

A reasonable time includes a reasonable time to investigate and assess the claim, and what counts as reasonable depends on the type of insurance, the size and complexity of the claim, and compliance with relevant statutory or regulatory rules.[2]

So a claim taking longer than you hoped is not automatically a breach. But the obligation is in the contract by operation of law, and asking an insurer to account for a delay is a reasonable thing to do. If you are not satisfied with how a claim is being handled, you can take a complaint to the Financial Ombudsman Service.[7]

Funeral costs before a claim is paid

Worth knowing early rather than discovering later.

Most firms in the regulator’s review said they are signed up to a form of funeral pledge, to help families who need to pay funeral costs where the claim cannot yet be settled because probate is unresolved.[1]

It is not automatic and it is not advertised. You have to ask.

Separately, a Funeral Expenses Payment is available to people receiving certain benefits who need help paying for a funeral. It will not usually cover all of the costs, and it is deducted from any money you receive from the estate.[5]

Making a claim: the practical steps

  • Find the policy documents, or any recent statement showing the insurer and policy number.
  • Contact the insurer. Most have a dedicated bereavement team.
  • Ask what they need before sending anything, so nothing goes back and forth twice.
  • Get several certified copies of the death certificate. You will need more than one.
  • Check whether the policy is in trust, and if so, who the trustees are, because they make the claim.
  • Use the Tell Us Once service to notify government departments together, remembering you will still need to tell banks, employers and pension providers separately.[5]
  • Keep a note of who you spoke to and when.

What makes a life insurance claim quicker

Almost everything that determines how quickly a claim is paid is settled when the policy is arranged, not when the claim is made.

Policies are typically set up once and then never looked at again. That is how families end up waiting on probate for a payout that could have gone straight to them, or hunting through paperwork for a policy nobody mentioned.

If you hold cover, five things are worth checking on your own policy:

  • Is it written in trust, or would it pay into your estate?
  • Is a beneficiary named, and is it still the person you would choose?[3]
  • Does someone you trust know the policy exists and where the documents are?
  • Was everything on the original application accurate and complete?
  • Does the amount and the term still match what your household would need?

None of that is urgent.

Things to keep in mind

  • The timescales here are averages across firms, not commitments by any insurer.[1]
  • If no beneficiary is named, the money goes into the estate and probate may be needed first.[3][6]
  • A claim may be reduced or refused if the information given when applying was not accurate.[4]
  • Insurers must pay sums due within a reasonable time, which includes time to investigate.[2]
  • Cover ends if premiums stop being paid.
