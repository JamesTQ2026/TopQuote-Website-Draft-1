The Rise of Wearable Health Tech

T
Tanweer Hussain
Protection Expert
29 July 2025 3 min read

Smartwatches, fitness bands, health apps, and wearable health tech are everywhere. A recent survey reported that one in three Brits is now regularly tracking everything from sleep to steps to heart rate, reflecting a growing interest in personal wellness data.

For many, these devices have become a daily habit. But with so much information on your wrist, it's easy to feel like you should be doing something about it all. Should you walk more? Sleep longer? Train harder? The truth is, there's no one-size-fits-all answer, and that is okay.

In this post, we are not telling you what to track or change. We are simply unpacking the rise of wearables and how you can engage with your data in a way that supports your overall wellbeing, without added pressure.

Why We Are All Tracking

The growing interest in health tracking has come alongside a wider movement toward self-care, prevention, and health awareness. Whether it's closing your daily movement rings or monitoring resting heart rate, data offers a feeling of control. For busy families juggling work, mortgages, and everything in between, it is one area where you can take charge. For some, it is motivating. For others, it can quickly become overwhelming.

The Metrics That Matter (And What They Mean)

Here is a breakdown of some common data points wearables track and what they can offer, and what they cannot:

  1. Steps
    A simple count of how much you move during the day. 10,000 steps is not a magic number; it is a general guideline. Movement of any kind adds up. A few short walks between meetings or school runs can be just as beneficial.
  2. Sleep Stages
    Many devices claim to track REM, deep, and light sleep. While this tech is still evolving, general trends, like total sleep time, can be useful to spot patterns over weeks rather than days. This is especially valuable when you are managing the demands of family life and career.
  3. Heart Rate and Heart Rate Variability (HRV)
    Resting heart rate can reflect general fitness and stress levels. HRV, the small variations between heartbeats, is a newer metric sometimes linked to stress and recovery. It is helpful to track this long-term rather than respond to daily fluctuations, particularly important when you are the one others depend on.

When Tracking Becomes Too Much

More data is not always better. Constantly checking your metrics can lead to stress or "data guilt", especially when the numbers do not go the way you expected. When you are already managing responsibilities at home and work, the last thing you need is another source of pressure. If you ever feel like your device is doing more harm than good, it is okay to take a break.

Health as a Long-Term Practice

At the heart of this trend is something positive, a shift toward tuning in to our bodies. These tools can support habits like movement, sleep, and self-care, things that make a real difference over time, especially when you are thinking about your long-term wellbeing and what that means for those closest to you.

And while we do not offer health advice, many of the clients we work with are on their own wellbeing journeys. Whether they are managing stress, establishing better routines, or simply trying to feel more in control of their health, tools like wearables can play a small role in that bigger picture of planning for the future.

Data as a Guide, Not a Goal

You do not need to hit every target your app suggests. Use your data as a guide, not a verdict. If your tracker helps you sleep better, move more, or understand your health in a new way, that is a win. But remember, your well-being is not measured in numbers alone. It is about being there for the people and commitments that matter most to you.
