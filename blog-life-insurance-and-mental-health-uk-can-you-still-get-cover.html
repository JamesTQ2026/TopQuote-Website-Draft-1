Life Insurance and Mental Health UK: Can You Still Get Cover?

T
Tanweer Hussain
Protection Expert
14 May 2026 6 min read

A diagnosis of depression, anxiety, or another mental health condition does not automatically disqualify you from obtaining life insurance in the UK. Insurers assess each application individually, weighing up a range of clinical and lifestyle factors. The outcome depends on what condition you have had, how severe it was, when it occurred, and whether you are still in treatment. Understanding how this process works puts you in a far stronger position when applying.

How Insurers Assess Mental Health History

When you apply for life insurance and disclose a mental health condition, the insurer will typically ask a series of underwriting questions. These are designed to build a picture of your condition and its likely impact on your long-term health. Common questions include:

  • What condition have you been diagnosed with?
  • When were you first diagnosed, and when did you last experience symptoms?
  • Have you ever been hospitalised or referred to a psychiatrist or specialist?
  • Are you currently taking medication, and if so, what type and at what dose?
  • Have you had any time off work due to your mental health?
  • Have you ever had any thoughts of self-harm or suicide?

These questions are not designed to discriminate against you. They are used to assess risk in the same way that insurers ask about physical health conditions such as heart disease or diabetes. The Equality Act 2010 provides important protections against unfair discrimination in the provision of financial services, and insurers must be able to justify any additional terms or declinatures with actuarial evidence.

What Factors Affect the Outcome

Several factors influence what terms you are offered, or whether you are offered cover at all.

Time since diagnosis or last episode: A single episode of mild depression that resolved five or more years ago is treated very differently from an ongoing condition requiring active management. The further away the episode, the more favourably it is likely to be viewed.

Severity of the condition: A diagnosis of generalised anxiety disorder managed with talking therapy is assessed differently from a severe depressive episode requiring hospitalisation or a condition involving psychosis. Insurers will pay close attention to whether a specialist was involved in your care.

Medication: Being prescribed antidepressants does not in itself lead to an automatic decline. However, the type of medication, the dose, and whether the prescription has changed recently will all be taken into account. A stable, low-dose prescription for a historic episode may have minimal impact on your application.

Hospitalisation or specialist referral: If your condition required inpatient treatment or a referral to a psychiatrist, this is likely to attract closer scrutiny. Insurers may request a medical report from your GP before making a decision.

Suicide risk: If a clinician has ever recorded concerns about self-harm or suicidal ideation, this will be a significant underwriting consideration. Some insurers will decline cover at this point, while others may offer cover with a suicide exclusion clause for a defined period.

How Well-Managed Conditions Are Viewed

Insurers recognise that mental health conditions, like many physical conditions, can be well-managed over the long term. A person who experienced a single episode of depression in their twenties, received treatment, recovered fully, and has had no recurrence for several years is unlikely to face significant additional terms. The key factors are stability, time elapsed, and the absence of ongoing specialist involvement.

If you are currently in active treatment, an insurer may ask you to reapply once your treatment is complete and you have been symptom-free for a period of time. Alternatively, they may offer cover with a loading on the premium, meaning you pay more than a person with no history of mental ill health.

Why a Whole-of-Market Broker Matters

Different insurers take different approaches to underwriting mental health conditions. Some are more cautious than others. Applying directly to a single insurer means you only see one set of underwriting criteria. A whole-of-market broker can assess your circumstances and identify which insurers are most likely to offer favourable terms, without requiring you to make multiple formal applications that could affect your record.

A specialist broker with experience in non-standard risk can also help you frame your application clearly and accurately, reducing the chance of questions or delays during underwriting. If your application is declined or loaded, a broker can help you understand why and what alternatives may be available.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will my life insurance application be automatically declined if I have depression?

No. A history of depression does not lead to an automatic decline. The outcome depends on the severity of the condition, how long ago it occurred, whether it has been resolved, and whether you required specialist treatment. Many people with a history of mild to moderate depression are offered standard or near-standard terms.

Do I have to disclose mental health conditions when applying for life insurance?

Yes. You are legally required to answer all application questions honestly and to disclose material facts. Failing to disclose a relevant medical history could result in your policy being voided and a claim being refused. Disclosure protects you as much as the insurer.

Can I get life insurance if I am currently taking antidepressants?

In many cases, yes. Being on medication does not automatically prevent you from obtaining cover. The type of medication, the dosage, and how stable your condition is will all be assessed. A specialist broker can help identify which insurers take the most favourable approach to your specific circumstances.

What is a mental health exclusion on a life insurance policy?

In some cases, particularly where there is a recent or ongoing history of self-harm or suicidal ideation, an insurer may offer a policy with a mental health exclusion. This means the policy will not pay out if death occurs as a direct result of the excluded condition. This is not universal and depends entirely on the individual underwriting assessment.
