Life Insurance with Pre-Existing Conditions UK: How Underwriting Works

T
Tanweer Hussain
Protection Expert
26 March 2026 5 min read

One of the most common questions people have about life insurance in the UK is whether a pre-existing medical condition affects their ability to get cover. The straightforward answer is that having a health history does not automatically prevent someone from obtaining life insurance with pre-existing conditions in the UK. What it means in practice is that the application goes through a more detailed assessment.

This article explains how underwriting works for applicants with a health history, what factors insurers typically look at, and what outcomes are possible. It is general information only and does not constitute financial advice.

What Is Underwriting for Life Insurance with Pre-Existing Conditions in the UK?

Underwriting is the process by which an insurer assesses the risk presented by an applicant. For a straightforward application from a healthy individual, this process is usually quick. For an applicant with a health history, underwriting involves gathering more detailed information about the specific condition, its management, and any associated risks.

The insurer uses this information to decide whether to offer cover, and if so, on what terms. Understanding that process can help applicants know what to expect.

What Insurers Typically Assess

When an applicant discloses a pre-existing condition, insurers will typically look at a range of factors. These commonly include the type and nature of the condition, when it was diagnosed, how it is currently managed, whether it is stable or progressive, any related complications or associated conditions, and the applicant's most recent test results or clinical measurements where relevant.

The weighting given to each of these factors varies by insurer and by condition. Different providers use different underwriting manuals and may reach different conclusions from the same set of facts.

Common Conditions and General Underwriting Approaches

Type 2 Diabetes

For well-controlled Type 2 diabetes, many insurers assess the application based on factors such as HbA1c levels, time since diagnosis, whether the condition is managed through diet or medication, and the presence of any complications. Outcomes vary considerably by insurer and by the individual's specific profile.

High Blood Pressure

Controlled hypertension where blood pressure is managed through medication or lifestyle changes is a relatively common finding in life insurance applications. Insurers typically look at recent blood pressure readings, current treatment, and whether there are any associated cardiovascular risk factors.

Mental Health Conditions

Applications involving a history of depression, anxiety or other mental health conditions are assessed based on factors such as severity, recency of episodes, whether hospitalisation has occurred, and current treatment. Outcomes range from standard terms to a premium loading depending on the individual circumstances.

Cancer History

Applications from people with a cancer history are among the more complex underwriting cases. Key factors include the type and stage of cancer, the time elapsed since treatment completed, and whether the applicant is currently in remission. Some insurers use specialist underwriters for these cases.

Possible Outcomes of Underwriting

There are several possible outcomes when an application involves a pre-existing condition:

  • Standard terms: the premium is the same as it would be for a standard application. This is possible for well-managed, lower-risk conditions.
  • Rated terms: the insurer offers cover but at a higher premium to reflect the additional risk. The loading can vary significantly between insurers for the same condition.
  • Exclusion: the insurer offers cover but excludes claims relating to the specific condition. The applicant is covered for all other causes of claim.
  • Postponement: the insurer is not willing to assess the application at this time, for example if a condition is very recent or under investigation, but may reassess at a later date.
  • Decline: the insurer is unable to offer cover. This is less common than many people expect, and a decline from one insurer does not mean all insurers will reach the same conclusion.

Why Outcomes Vary Between Insurers

Different insurers use different underwriting criteria for the same condition. One provider may offer standard terms for a condition that another rates or excludes. This variation is one of the reasons why accessing a range of insurers is relevant for applicants with a health history. A declined or rated application from one insurer does not reflect the position across the whole market.

The Association of British Insurers provides general guidance on life insurance and what to expect when applying.

The Importance of Accurate Disclosure

Regardless of the condition involved, accurate and complete disclosure on an application is essential. Failure to disclose a material fact, even unintentionally, can result in a claim being declined on the grounds of non-disclosure. The insurer may void the policy if it later discovers that relevant information was withheld at the time of application.

The FCA's rules on insurance applications require that all material information is disclosed. Further information is available from the Financial Conduct Authority.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Does a previous decline from one insurer affect future applications?

A decline from one insurer does not appear on a credit file and does not create a formal record that other insurers can access. Each insurer makes its own underwriting decision based on the information provided in the application. However, some applications ask whether cover has previously been declined or offered on non-standard terms, and this should be answered honestly.

Q: Can I get life insurance with a pre-existing condition if I have had no recent treatment?

The recency and current status of a condition is one of the factors insurers consider. A condition that was treated historically and has been stable or resolved for a number of years may be assessed differently from a condition that is recent or ongoing.
